Devanahalli Farmers Prepare for Indefinite Protest: Why Is Karnataka Seeking 1,200 Acres Near Foxconn?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Wed, 28 Oct 2026 - 14:11
Developing

Farmers across five villages are preparing for an indefinite protest against a proposed land acquisition near Foxconn’s facility in North Bengaluru. With 369 acres already covered by a preliminary notification and another 831 acres reportedly being planned, the dispute raises questions about farmland, water resources and whether Karnataka has considered alternatives before acquiring more land.

The land-acquisition dispute around Doddaballapur and Devanahalli is growing. What began with a preliminary notification covering 369 acres in two villages has now become a wider concern for farmers across five villages, as the Karnataka Industrial Areas Development Board (KIADB) reportedly plans to acquire a total of 1,200 acres near the Foxconn facility.

Farmers from Sonnamaranahalli and Dandudasa Kodigehalli in Doddaballapur taluk, along with Koira, Mayasandra and Meesaganahalli in Devanahalli taluk, are preparing for an indefinite protest. Village-level meetings are being held to mobilise support, following an earlier demonstration at the Bengaluru Rural Deputy Commissioner’s office attended by more than 1,000 people, according to reports.

The latest development comes after our earlier article, Doddaballapur Farmers Protest Land Acquisition: Should Karnataka Use Unused Industrial Land Before Taking Farmland?, examined the concerns raised by farmers over the proposed acquisition of 369 acres.

The new development is significant because the proposed extent is reportedly much larger. The preliminary notification issued on October 12 covers 369 acres in Sonnamaranahalli and Dandudasa Kodigehalli. The additional 831 acres are reportedly being planned across Koira, Mayasandra and Meesaganahalli.

The distinction matters: the 369 acres have been covered by a preliminary notification, while the additional 831 acres should not be described as formally notified for acquisition unless the relevant notifications are issued. The full 1,200-acre proposal should therefore not be treated as a completed land acquisition.

Why are farmers preparing for an indefinite protest now? Their concern is not limited to the loss of individual plots. They fear that further industrial expansion could reduce the agricultural land available to families who depend on farming for their livelihoods.

Farmers have also pointed to the experience of neighbouring villages, where they say substantial areas of fertile land have already been acquired for industrial development in recent years. Some residents fear that if they do not organise now, they could face a similar situation.

The proposed expansion also raises a question for the Karnataka government: why is additional agricultural land needed, and what assessment has been made of industrial land that has already been acquired but remains unused or undeveloped?

Karnataka has reasons to attract manufacturing investment. Industrial projects can create employment, support local businesses and strengthen the state’s economy. Foxconn’s presence has also contributed to growing industrial interest in the region.

But attracting investment should not remove the need to examine how much land is genuinely required, whether existing industrial areas can accommodate further development and what the consequences will be for the people living in the affected villages.

Before acquiring more farmland, the government should publicly explain whether it has assessed vacant industrial plots, the progress of earlier land acquisitions and the possibility of using land that has already been earmarked for industrial purposes. If those alternatives are unsuitable, the reasons should be made clear.

The question is not whether Karnataka should welcome industries. It is whether industrial growth must continue to depend on acquiring more productive agricultural land without first demonstrating that available alternatives are insufficient.

The controversy has also drawn political attention. Social Welfare Minister K. H. Muniyappa, who represents the area, has written to Chief Minister D. K. Shivakumar and Industries Minister M. B. Patil, urging them to abandon the proposed acquisition.

Muniyappa reportedly pointed out that more than 9,000 acres had already been acquired in Devanahalli taluk for the international airport and industrial projects. His intervention raises a question about whether the region has reached a point where further acquisition needs especially careful scrutiny.

However, a minister’s request to drop the proposal does not itself mean the government has cancelled it. The government’s response remains important: will it reconsider the plan, explain why the additional land is required, or proceed with the acquisition process?

The farmers’ concerns also extend beyond agriculture to the environment. Residents have alleged that the proposed land falls within the Arkavathi river catchment area and fear that industrial development could affect local water sources.

These concerns deserve examination, but they need to be assessed against maps, land records, hydrological studies and applicable environmental rules. A claim that land falls within a river catchment does not, by itself, establish that every proposed industrial activity would be illegal or necessarily damage the river.

The government should therefore publish or make available the relevant environmental assessments and explain how the proposed industrial area would affect groundwater recharge, drainage, agricultural water use and the wider catchment. If the land is environmentally sensitive, that should be considered before decisions become difficult to reverse.

For farmers, the immediate issue is whether they will retain their land and livelihoods. For nearby residents, it is whether industrial expansion could affect water availability and the surrounding environment. For Karnataka, it is whether investment policy can balance employment and economic growth with the long-term interests of rural communities.

The authorities must also explain the acquisition process to affected landowners, including the survey numbers involved, the legal procedure for raising objections, how compensation would be calculated and what safeguards apply to those whose land is selected.

The public should not have to rely only on competing statements from officials, politicians and protesters to understand a proposal of this scale. The relevant notifications, land-use details and reasons for the proposed expansion should be accessible and clearly explained.

An indefinite protest is a sign that the dispute risks becoming more serious, but the government still has an opportunity to address the concerns through transparency and meaningful consultation. A response that simply treats farmers as opponents of development would miss the central issue: people want to know why their land is needed and whether their concerns have been considered fairly.

Karnataka’s industrial ambitions and farmers’ livelihoods do not have to be treated as opposing interests. The state can pursue investment while examining unused industrial land, assessing environmental risks and explaining the need for every additional acre it proposes to acquire.

The immediate questions remain unanswered. Why has the proposed extent expanded from 369 acres to 1,200 acres? What alternatives have been examined? Will the government respond to Muniyappa’s request? And what evidence will establish the impact of the proposed development on the Arkavathi river catchment?

Until these questions receive clear answers, the proposed acquisition will remain a matter of public concern for the affected villages and for everyone interested in how North Bengaluru develops.

Industrial development should create opportunities for Karnataka without making farmers feel that the future of their land has already been decided without them.

The farmers are preparing for an indefinite protest. Whether that becomes necessary may now depend on how the government responds.
