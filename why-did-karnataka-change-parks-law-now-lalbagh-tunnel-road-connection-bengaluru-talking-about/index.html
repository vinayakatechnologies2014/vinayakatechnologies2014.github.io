Why Did Karnataka Change the Parks Law Now? The Lalbagh–Tunnel Road Connection Bengaluru Is Talking About

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Fri, 28 Aug 2026 - 14:02
Public Buzz

A change to Karnataka's law protecting government parks has suddenly become a subject of public discussion in Bengaluru.

The reason is not just the much-discussed 5% provision in the new amendment. It is the timing of the change and its connection to a major infrastructure project involving Lalbagh Botanical Garden.

The sequence is what has caught people's attention:

A tunnel project has a documented land requirement associated with Lalbagh → Lalbagh is protected under the existing parks law → Karnataka proposes an amendment allowing limited alienation of government parkland for public infrastructure → the Legislature passes the amendment.

That sequence does not, by itself, prove that the amendment was introduced specifically for the tunnel project. But it explains why Bengaluru residents, environmental groups and others are asking questions about the connection.

What is the tunnel project?

Bengaluru has been planning a large underground road project connecting the Hebbal side of the city with the Central Silk Board area.

The project is intended to create a major north-south transport corridor and provide an alternative route through some of Bengaluru's busiest areas.

The detailed project documents include tunnel and ramp sections around Lalbagh. The project documentation filed before the National Green Tribunal records a requirement involving Lalbagh land, including an area of around six acres associated with the proposed works. It identifies Lalbagh Botanical Gardens as a government park maintained by the Department of Horticulture and protected under the Karnataka Government Parks (Preservation) Act, 1975.

This is where the legal question enters the picture.

Why does Lalbagh's legal status matter?

Lalbagh is not simply vacant government land.

It is a government park covered by the Karnataka Government Parks (Preservation) Act, 1975. The Act was created specifically to provide for the preservation of certain government parks in Karnataka.

The law has also been amended in the past to permit specific uses of portions of protected parks for infrastructure.

For example, the existing version of the Act records an earlier provision allowing 1,135.18 square metres of Lalbagh land along the western side of the park to be transferred to Bangalore Metro Rail Corporation Limited for construction of an elevated metro station.

So there is already a history of infrastructure requirements leading to specific changes involving protected parkland.

The current proposal is broader because the 2026 amendment creates a general mechanism for limited alienation of government parkland for specified public infrastructure and public utility purposes, rather than naming only a particular parcel for a particular project.

What has Karnataka changed now?

The Karnataka Legislature has passed the Karnataka Government Parks (Preservation) (Amendment) Bill, 2026.

The amendment introduces a provision under which up to 5% of the total area of a government park or garden can be considered for alienation for specified public infrastructure and public utility purposes, subject to the conditions contained in the amendment.

The important point is that this does not mean five per cent of every park has automatically been handed over for construction.

The provision creates a legal mechanism for considering limited parkland use for qualifying public purposes.

It also establishes conditions and an evaluation process for such proposals.

The Karnataka Legislature passed the amendment during its August 2026 session.

So, is the 5% amendment specifically for the tunnel project?

This is the question at the centre of much of the public discussion.

The timing is certainly notable.

The tunnel project has a documented land requirement involving Lalbagh, while the existing parks legislation provides protection to government parkland.

The new amendment creates a mechanism for limited alienation of such land for public infrastructure.

Those are three independently established facts.

But they do not automatically establish a fourth fact:

That the government introduced the amendment specifically to clear the tunnel project.

That conclusion would require documentary evidence showing that the tunnel project's land requirement was a reason for introducing or passing the amendment.

Without such evidence, it is more accurate to say that the amendment has become closely associated in public discussion with the Lalbagh tunnel-road issue because of the timing and the overlap between the project’s land requirements and the subject of the amended law.

What is fact and what remains a question?

At this point, the basic sequence is clear.

Fact: The Hebbal–Central Silk Board tunnel project's planning documents identify land requirements associated with Lalbagh, including an area of around six acres connected with the proposed works.

Fact: Lalbagh is protected under the Karnataka Government Parks (Preservation) Act, 1975.

Fact: Karnataka has passed an amendment creating a mechanism for limited alienation of government parkland for specified public infrastructure and public utility purposes.

Fact: The amendment and the Lalbagh tunnel project have therefore become connected in public discussion.

Still a question: Was the amendment introduced specifically to enable the tunnel project?

That last question should not be presented as an established fact unless official documents directly establish it.

For Bengaluru, however, the timing is enough to make the issue worth watching.

The 5% provision may eventually apply to several types of public infrastructure across Karnataka. But because Lalbagh is already part of a major tunnel-road proposal, the first real test of how the new provision works could become particularly important for Bengaluru.

For now, the question people are asking is simple:

Did Karnataka change the parks law because Bengaluru's infrastructure needs were running into the existing legal protection for parkland — and what will happen to Lalbagh if that new legal route is used?
