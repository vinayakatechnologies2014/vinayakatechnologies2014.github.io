SIT’s 7,005-Page Final Report in Crime No. 39/2025, Registered at Dharmasthala Police Station

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Fri, 11 Sep 2026 - 09:08
Developing

The recovery of human skeletal remains at Banglegudde, Dharmasthala has left behind questions that cannot be answered simply by identifying the bones.

Who were these people? How did they die? When did they die? How did their remains reach the location? And, most importantly, if any of these deaths were the result of a crime, who was responsible?

The Special Investigation Team’s (SIT) 7,005-page final report in Crime No. 39/2025, registered at Dharmasthala Police Station, contains forensic and investigative material that may help answer some of those questions. Portions of the report dealing with the Banglegudde recovery reportedly record DNA matches involving skeletal remains, including remains associated with identified individuals.

The findings raise a fundamental issue that goes beyond simply identifying the dead: the circumstances surrounding their deaths and the presence of their remains at Banglegudde.

DNA matches raise questions about the identities of the dead

According to the forensic findings reported from the SIT material, skeletal remains recovered at Banglegudde were subjected to DNA profiling. The Bengaluru Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL) reportedly provided a relevant DNA examination report to the SIT on June 23, 2026. The reported findings include DNA matches involving several recovered bone samples.

Among them:

  • A group of bone samples reportedly produced an autosomal DNA profile matching the blood sample of Shrinivasa, son of Krishnappa.
  • Bone sample 8b reportedly matched the blood sample of Padma, wife of late Gangadhara.
  • Bone samples 29a, 29b and 29c reportedly matched the blood sample of Uluvangada Jeevan, son of late U.B. Ayyappa.
  • Several other bone samples reportedly produced identical DNA profiles.
  • A number of recovered bones were reportedly identified as human and male.
  • Item No. 20 was reportedly identified as human and female.

These findings are important because they potentially give investigators something more concrete than unidentified skeletal remains: identities that can be traced back to real people and their families. That opens the door to the next set of questions.

  • Were these individuals reported missing?
  • When were they last seen?
  • What was known about their deaths?
  • Were their deaths already recorded as natural, accidental or otherwise?
  • And how did their remains come to be at Banglegudde?

Those questions are central to determining whether the recovered remains are connected to any criminal activity.

An ulna reportedly found inside a skull

Another reported finding from the SIT material is particularly unusual.

A forearm bone known as an ulna was reportedly found inserted into a human skull recovered at Banglegudde. The material reportedly identifies the item as Article No. 90 — “Ulna inserted in the skull.” The recovery was reportedly made at Spot No. 1 at Banglegudde on September 17, 2025.

The investigation also reportedly recovered a driving licence bearing the name Adi Shesha Narayana, son of Bojanna, from Dasarahalli in Gubbi taluk of Tumakuru. A woman identified as Padma reportedly told investigators that the licence belonged to her brother, Adi Shesha Narayana. DNA material from skeletal remains was reportedly compared with Padma's sample and produced a match. The unusual positioning of the ulna inside the skull does not, by itself, establish how or why it happened. But it is precisely the kind of finding that demands an explanation.

Was the positioning caused by natural processes, the movement of remains, disturbance of the burial site, or deliberate human action?

The forensic evidence may help answer those questions.

Finding the dead is only the beginning

The identification of skeletal remains is an important step in an investigation. But identification is not the end of the investigation.

If the remains belong to people whose deaths have unresolved circumstances, investigators need to establish what happened to them before their remains were recovered. A DNA match can establish identity or a genetic association. It does not, by itself, establish murder.

What investigators need to establish?

The crucial questions surrounding the Banglegudde remains include:

  • Who exactly do the recovered remains belong to?
  • Were the individuals reported missing?
  • When were they last known to be alive?
  • What was the recorded cause and manner of their deaths?
  • When did they die?
  • Where did they die?
  • How did their remains reach Banglegudde?
  • Who had access to the location?
  • Is there evidence that the remains were deliberately moved or placed there?
  • Do witness statements, phone records, forensic evidence or other material connect any individual to the deaths or disposal of remains?
  • If a criminal act is established, who was responsible?

These are the questions that can turn forensic findings into an understanding of what actually happened.

The discovery and identification of human remains should not be treated as the conclusion of the story.

For families and the public, the search for answers goes further — from identification to circumstances, from circumstances to responsibility, and from responsibility to accountability where the evidence supports it.

If the evidence establishes that criminal acts were committed, those responsible must be identified and dealt with through due process of law. Until that evidence is established, individuals should not be labelled as perpetrators merely on the basis of suspicion or association.
