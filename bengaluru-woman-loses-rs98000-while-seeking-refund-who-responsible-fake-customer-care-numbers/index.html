Bengaluru Woman Loses ₹98,000 While Seeking a Refund. Who Is Responsible for Fake Customer-Care Numbers?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Tue, 27 Oct 2026 - 08:18
Opinion

A damaged grocery delivery led to an alleged cyber fraud. The case raises questions about online search results, quick-commerce safeguards and how quickly banks can respond when money disappears.

A damaged packet of curd should have been a minor consumer complaint. For a 56-year-old woman in Bengaluru, however, an attempt to obtain a refund reportedly ended in a loss of ₹98,000 within about a minute.

The woman, a resident of Vasanth Nagar, had ordered curd through Blinkit on October 17. After receiving the damaged packet, she tried to find the company’s customer-care number online. According to the police complaint, a person who answered her call sent her a link through WhatsApp and asked her to upload a photograph of the damaged product.

A second caller later claimed that the refund was being processed and instructed her to check her digital payment account. The woman subsequently discovered that ₹98,000 had been debited from her Bank of Baroda account between approximately 3.15 pm and 3.16 pm.

She approached Bengaluru’s Central Cyber Crime police station on October 23. Police registered a case involving cheating and impersonation and began investigating.

The available details do not establish exactly how the money was taken, who operated the numbers or whether the suspects have been identified. Those questions must be answered through the investigation. But the incident raises a broader concern: why can an ordinary customer searching for help after a damaged delivery end up speaking to someone allegedly attempting to defraud them?

The danger is not limited to grocery deliveries. Customers routinely search online for contact numbers when they have problems with banking services, courier deliveries, online shopping, travel bookings, utility bills and digital payments. A person trying to resolve a genuine problem may not know whether a number displayed in search results belongs to the company or to someone impersonating it.

That creates an important question for search platforms: what safeguards help users distinguish official customer-support information from potentially fraudulent numbers?

Search results can include information published by third parties, and the appearance of a phone number in a search does not automatically establish that it is genuine. However, consumers may reasonably interpret a prominent result as trustworthy, particularly when they are in a hurry to resolve a problem.

Platforms need accessible ways to report fraudulent contact details, effective processes for reviewing reports and appropriate action when deceptive information is identified. Where a number is not verified, users should not be left with the impression that its presence in search results proves its authenticity.

The Bengaluru case also raises questions for quick-commerce companies. When a customer receives a damaged or spoiled product, the official complaint and refund process should be easy to locate, simple to use and clear enough that customers do not feel compelled to search elsewhere for assistance.

If a customer can raise a complaint directly through an app, that route should be prominently explained. Companies should also make it easy to identify their legitimate support channels and warn customers about unsolicited calls, external links and requests for banking credentials or payment authorisation.

These questions do not mean Blinkit caused the alleged fraud. The available information does not establish that. The issue is whether online services can reduce the opportunities for impersonation and make safe support channels easier to find.

Banks and payment providers have another crucial role. Once money is transferred or withdrawn through a fraudulent interaction, the speed of reporting and response can matter. Customers need clear instructions for reporting unauthorised transactions, blocking compromised access and seeking assistance with potentially recoverable funds.

When a customer reports fraud, how quickly can the bank act? What transaction-monitoring safeguards are available? How effectively can banks coordinate with payment providers and law-enforcement agencies to trace and potentially freeze funds?

These are questions worth examining without assuming that a bank is responsible simply because an account was debited. The method used in this case remains under investigation, and the available details do not establish whether any particular security control failed.

The incident is also not necessarily an isolated warning. A similar case was reported in Bengaluru in March, when a 65-year-old woman allegedly lost ₹77,000 while trying to return spoiled milk purchased online. The circumstances of individual cases can differ, but repeated incidents involving ordinary refund requests deserve closer scrutiny.

For consumers, a few precautions can reduce the risk. Raise complaints through the service provider’s official app or verified website rather than relying on unverified numbers found online. Be cautious about links sent by unknown callers, and never disclose your UPI PIN, banking password or OTP to someone claiming to process a refund. Do not approve an unexplained payment request or transaction merely because someone says it is necessary to receive money back.

If money is debited unexpectedly, contact the bank immediately and report the suspected cyber fraud through India’s 1930 cybercrime helpline and the official National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal. Prompt reporting may help authorities and financial institutions take action, although recovery is not guaranteed.

The larger issue is not whether consumers should exercise caution. They should. It is whether the digital systems people depend on make safe choices practical and unsafe impersonation harder.

A customer seeking a refund should not have to become an expert in identifying fraudulent phone numbers, suspicious links and deceptive payment instructions. A safer digital marketplace requires more than warnings to consumers. It requires easily identifiable official support channels, responsive reporting systems, effective safeguards and timely action when fraud is reported.

The investigation must establish who took the money and how. Beyond that, the case leaves a public-interest question for search platforms, quick-commerce companies, banks and payment providers: when a routine request for customer support becomes an opportunity for fraud, what more can each of them do to prevent the next customer from suffering the same loss?
