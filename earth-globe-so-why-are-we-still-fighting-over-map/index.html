The Earth Is a Globe. So Why Are We Still Fighting Over the Map?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Thu, 10 Sep 2026 - 11:52
Opinion

The United Nations has just taken up a debate that sounds simple but becomes surprisingly political: how the world should be represented on a map.

On September 4, the UN General Assembly adopted the “Correct the Map” resolution, an initiative led by Togo and the African Union. The argument is that commonly used map projections can make some parts of the world appear much larger or smaller than they really are, particularly making Africa look smaller relative to regions farther from the Equator. The resolution encourages the use of equal-area projections when relative size matters, but does not ban existing projections.

Africa has a legitimate point about geographical distortion. But here's what I don't understand:

Why are we treating one flat picture of a round planet as though it can ever be the single correct representation of the world?

The Earth is a globe. Every time we flatten that globe onto paper or a screen, something has to give. One projection may preserve direction better, another may preserve relative area, while another may make shapes easier to recognise. There is no perfect flat map. So instead of fighting over which map is “correct”, why not simply keep different maps for different purposes? Show an equal-area map when comparing the size of continents. Use another projection when navigation or other geographical purposes require it. Explain the limitations instead of pretending one map can represent everything perfectly.

The issue becomes even more important when we move from geography to political borders. A line on a map can look simple, but what happens when two countries claim the same territory?

Take India & China. If a map simply draws one line without explaining that the boundary is disputed, an ordinary reader can easily assume that the line represents an uncontested international border — Why not just say that it is disputed?

Interestingly, the UN already has a precedent for doing exactly that. Its own cartographic guidance gives the India-China border as an example where the UN recognises neither claim and has shown both the Indian- and Chinese-claimed borderlines, accompanied by a disclaimer that the depiction does not imply UN endorsement or acceptance.

That, to me, is far more sensible than pretending every border on a world map is equally settled.

Don't quietly choose one country's version. Mark the disputed area. Explain the competing claims. Tell the reader what is established and what remains contested.

And this is why the current “Correct the Map” debate feels incomplete to me. If the international community is going to discuss how maps shape people's understanding of the world, then the discussion should not stop at whether Africa looks too small or Greenland looks too large. It should also address how maps represent uncertainty. The UN itself acknowledged during the debate that the resolution does not affect sovereignty, territorial status, delimitation or internationally recognised borders.

So why not take the idea further? Instead of searching for one supposedly “correct” world map, the UN could maintain different official representations for different purposes — and, wherever borders are disputed, clearly mark that dispute rather than making the map appear more certain than reality.

Because Africa does not become smaller because a projection makes it look smaller. Greenland does not become larger because it appears larger on a screen. And a disputed territory does not automatically become one country's territory because a line has been printed around it.

A map is a representation of reality. It is not reality itself.

The Earth is already there. It is round. It does not need to be corrected.
