Supreme Court Awards ₹30 Lakh to Man Jailed for Seven Years After Acquittal; Calls for Law to Compensate Wrongful Incarceration

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Tue, 17 Nov 2026 - 11:23
Public Buzz

A man who remained in prison for more than seven years despite being acquitted in a murder case has been awarded ₹30 lakh in compensation by the Supreme Court, bringing renewed attention to the consequences of wrongful incarceration and failures in the criminal justice system.

The case concerns Devilal Gond from Chhattisgarh, who was arrested on December 7, 2012, in connection with a murder case. A Sessions Court convicted him on August 14, 2013, and sentenced him to life imprisonment. However, the Chhattisgarh High Court overturned the conviction and acquitted him on January 20, 2018.

Despite the acquittal, Gond continued to remain in prison. His release came only on May 19, 2025, after he had spent another seven years, three months and 29 days behind bars. The State did not challenge the High Court's acquittal, which had attained finality.

The failure to release him was reportedly caused by a communication gap between the prison authorities and the legal-aid system. The jail superintendent had sought information about the status of Gond's appeal from the concerned panel lawyer in April and June 2018. However, the information about his acquittal apparently did not reach the prison authorities.

The situation came to light in May 2025 during a verification exercise involving prisoners' records. By then, Gond had spent years in custody despite the judicial order setting aside his conviction and sentence.

The Supreme Court criticised the institutional failure, describing the authorities' conduct as showing complete remissness. The case exposed how a judicial order, even after becoming final, can fail to protect an individual's liberty when the institutions responsible for implementing it do not communicate effectively.

Gond had earlier received ₹2 lakh in compensation from the Chhattisgarh High Court. He approached the Supreme Court seeking enhanced compensation for the years he had lost after his acquittal. The Supreme Court increased the amount to ₹30 lakh, recognising the serious consequences of keeping a person in prison without a valid basis for continued detention.

The impact of those additional years cannot be measured only in terms of the time spent behind bars. Gond was around 28 years old when the High Court acquitted him and approximately 35 when he was finally released. The lost years affected his opportunities to earn a livelihood, pursue education and develop skills, in addition to depriving him of personal freedom and family life.

The judgment also raises a larger question about the absence of a comprehensive legal framework in India for compensating people who suffer wrongful prosecution or unlawful incarceration. The Supreme Court referred to the Law Commission's 277th Report, which examined the issue and recommended a framework for compensation and rehabilitation.

The Court's observations highlight the need for a clear mechanism that determines when compensation should be awarded, how the loss suffered by an individual should be assessed and what remedies should be available when government authorities fail to respect a person's legal rights.

Importantly, compensation after such an incident does not restore the years a person has lost. It can, however, recognise the harm caused and establish accountability for institutional failures. Without clear rules and procedures, people who experience similar situations may have to approach courts individually to seek relief.

The Supreme Court also called for improvements in communication across the criminal justice system. It directed High Courts to consider establishing an Online Criminal Justice Ecosystem to improve coordination between police, courts, prisons, prosecution agencies, forensic institutions and hospitals. Such a system is intended to help ensure that important judicial orders, including acquittal and release orders, reach the authorities responsible for implementing them without unnecessary delay.

The case is not merely about one person's compensation. It exposes a fundamental weakness in a system where an acquittal can be delivered by a court but remain ineffective for the person concerned because the order is not properly communicated or acted upon.

When a court declares a person acquitted, the responsibility of the system does not end with delivering the judgment. The authorities must ensure that the person is released without delay, unless another lawful reason exists for continued detention.

The larger test will be whether this judgment leads to a legal framework for compensating wrongful incarceration and practical safeguards that prevent similar failures. A person's liberty cannot depend on whether one government office successfully communicates with another.
