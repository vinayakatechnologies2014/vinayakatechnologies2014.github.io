Parole and Furlough in India: Where Should the Law Draw the Line?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Tue, 25 Aug 2026 - 12:01
Opinion

Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh's 17th temporary release raises a larger question: when does a legal provision meant for temporary relief become excessive freedom for a convicted prisoner?

Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh has walked out of prison again.

The Dera Sacha Sauda chief, serving a 20-year sentence for the rape of two women disciples, has been granted 21 days of furlough. It is his 17th temporary release since 2020.

The latest release has once again brought India's parole and furlough system under scrutiny.

The bigger question is not only why Ram Rahim was released.

It is:

Are India's parole and furlough rules too lenient when applied repeatedly to serious convicts?

What Are Parole and Furlough?

Parole and furlough allow eligible prisoners to leave prison temporarily under specific legal conditions.

They do not cancel a conviction or sentence.

In Haryana, the system is governed by the Haryana Good Conduct Prisoners (Temporary Release) Act, 2022.

The issue is not whether the law permits temporary release.

It does.

The issue is how often it should be permitted for the same serious offender.

Seventeen Releases: Is That Too Many?

Ram Rahim's latest release is his 17th temporary release since 2020.

That includes both parole and furlough periods of varying lengths.

One release may be justified by the applicable rules.

But repeated releases raise a different question:

Should a prisoner's previous temporary releases affect the decision on the next one?

If someone serving a long sentence repeatedly spends periods outside prison, the public can reasonably question whether the original punishment is being diluted.

Ram Rahim Is Not the Only Example

Ram Rahim's repeated temporary releases are unusual, but they are not without precedent.

Delhi High Court records show a life convict who had received 5 paroles and 9 furloughs, with 29 temporary releases recorded as incident-free.

In another case, involving a prisoner convicted of the rape and murder of a 12-year-old girl, the court recorded 3 paroles and 8 furloughs. The authorities had opposed his premature release, citing the gravity of the offence and other concerns.

Another Delhi High Court judgment records a prisoner who had been released at least 10 times on parole and furlough without misusing his temporary liberty.

The Bombay High Court has also dealt with furlough in the case of Arun Gawli, considering his previous conduct and whether earlier temporary liberty had been misused.

These cases show that repeated temporary release is not unique to Ram Rahim. But they also show that previous conduct, the seriousness of the offence, police objections and the circumstances of each prisoner can influence the decision.

The bigger question remains:

Should there be stricter limits or additional scrutiny when a serious convict repeatedly seeks temporary release?

Does Repeated Release Need Stricter Rules?

Furlough is linked to good conduct under Haryana law, but should good behaviour automatically justify repeated periods outside prison?

For a serious offender serving a long sentence, previous releases, the nature of the offence and public-safety concerns should all matter before another release is granted.

The Haryana law allows temporary release to be refused where authorities are satisfied that it could affect public order, State security or create a reasonable apprehension of a breach of peace.

This becomes particularly important when the prisoner has a large public following or has been convicted of a serious sexual or violent offence.

Should such prisoners face higher scrutiny after repeated releases?

Possible safeguards could include:

  • Stricter review after multiple releases
  • Limits on repeated temporary release
  • Enhanced public-safety assessment
  • Stronger conditions for high-risk prisoners
  • Independent review of repeated releases

The aim is not to eliminate parole or furlough.

It is to ensure that temporary release remains an exception and does not gradually become a recurring privilege.

Should India Have a National Parole and Furlough Database?

There is no simple public system through which citizens can compare parole and furlough decisions across all Indian states.

A national, anonymised database could record:

  • offence and sentence;
  • number of previous releases;
  • duration;
  • grounds for release;
  • conditions imposed;
  • and violations, if any.

That would make it easier to answer an important question:

Are influential prisoners receiving temporary release more frequently than similarly placed ordinary prisoners?

Transparency would make the system easier to trust — and easier to challenge when something appears inconsistent.

The Bigger Law & Justice Question

Ram Rahim's 17th temporary release should not automatically be called illegal.

But neither should repeated releases be accepted without scrutiny simply because the law allows temporary release.

The justice system should ask:

  • How many times is too many?
  • Should serious offenders face stricter conditions?
  • Should previous releases affect future eligibility?
  • Should victims' interests receive greater weight?
  • Should high-profile prisoners face additional public-order scrutiny?

And most importantly:

Does a long prison sentence still have meaning if temporary release becomes a recurring privilege?

Parole and furlough may have a place in a modern prison system.

But for serious crimes, temporary freedom should remain the exception — not become an expectation.

That is where India's Law & Justice system needs to draw the line.
