Coal in India: From the First Mines to Its Role in the Country's Energy System Today

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Wed, 21 Oct 2026 - 08:57
Opinion

Coal has played an important role in India's economic and industrial development for more than two centuries. It powered steam locomotives, supported the growth of factories, supplied fuel to steel and cement production, and became the principal source of electricity generation as the country expanded its power infrastructure.

Its role has changed over time, shaped by industrial development, government policy, technological progress and rising energy demand. Understanding this history provides a clearer picture of how coal became central to India's energy system and how it fits into the country's energy mix today.

Commercial coal mining in India began in 1774, when John Sumner and Suetonius Grant Heatly initiated mining activity in the Raniganj coalfield in present-day West Bengal. They obtained permission to work coal deposits during the early period of commercial coal development in British India. Early production was limited, but demand gradually increased as transport and industry developed.

A major change came with the introduction of steam locomotives in India in 1853. Railways needed substantial quantities of coal to power locomotives, creating a more consistent market for domestic coal. Mining expanded, and coal became increasingly important to railway transport, industrial operations and other commercial activities. According to the Ministry of Coal, annual coal production reached approximately six million tonnes by 1900 and 30 million tonnes by 1946.

Following Independence in 1947, coal became an important resource for India's planned economic development. The country needed energy to expand railways, establish factories, develop steel production and build infrastructure. Coal was available domestically in significant quantities and could supply large industrial facilities and power stations.

The government began taking a more active role in coal development. The National Coal Development Corporation was established in 1956 to expand production and develop coal resources. During the early decades after Independence, coal continued to support railway operations and industrial activity, while the expansion of electricity generation created another major source of demand.

Government control over the coal industry increased during the 1970s. Coking coal mines were nationalised in 1971–72, followed by non-coking coal mines in 1973. Coal India Limited was established in 1975 to manage major coal-mining operations. These changes shaped the structure of India's coal industry for decades.

As India's electricity network expanded, coal became increasingly important for power generation. Coal-fired thermal power stations could generate large amounts of electricity using fuel produced within the country. Electricity demand grew alongside population growth, urbanisation, agricultural activity, manufacturing and household electrification. Coal consequently became the backbone of India's grid-based electricity supply.

Coal also continued to be used directly by industries. In conventional steelmaking, coking coal is processed into coke, which plays an important role in blast furnaces by providing heat and helping remove oxygen from iron ore. However, India imports substantial quantities of coking coal because domestic supplies do not fully meet the steel industry's requirements for suitable grades and quality. The Ministry of Coal reported coking coal imports of approximately 57.58 million tonnes in 2024–25. Cement plants use coal and other fuels to provide the heat required for clinker production, while other industrial processes use coal for heating and steam generation. These uses are distinct from coal burned at power stations to produce electricity.

India's coal production increased substantially over the decades. The Ministry of Coal reported production of approximately 1,047.5 million tonnes in 2024–25, reflecting the scale of the country's domestic coal industry. Coal India remains a major producer, alongside other public and private sector mining operations.

Coal's importance is also visible in electricity-generation figures. Central Electricity Authority statistics show that coal and lignite generated approximately 1,331.9 billion units of electricity in 2024–25, accounting for around 73 per cent of utility electricity generation in the figures reported for that year. This demonstrates the continuing importance of coal to India's electricity supply, even as other sources have expanded.

Over time, India's energy system has diversified. Hydropower has contributed to electricity generation for decades, while nuclear power has developed as another source of grid electricity. Natural gas is used in power generation and industry. Solar and wind power have expanded significantly in recent years, supported by changes in technology, investment and government policy.

These sources serve different roles. Solar power generates electricity when sunlight is available, wind power depends on wind conditions, hydropower uses flowing or stored water, and nuclear power generates electricity through nuclear fission. Natural gas can supply electricity and industrial heat, while petroleum products remain important in transport. Traditional biomass and modern bioenergy also contribute to energy use in different parts of the country.

Household energy use has evolved alongside these changes. Electricity is now used for lighting, fans, refrigeration and many appliances. LPG is widely used for cooking, while some households use electricity, biogas, biomass or other fuels depending on location, affordability and access. Most households do not burn coal directly for everyday energy needs, but electricity consumed in their homes may still be generated at coal-fired power stations.

Industrial energy use is more varied. Some industries consume electricity generated from the national grid, while others use coal, petroleum products, natural gas, biomass or other fuels directly. The particular mix depends on the industrial process, available infrastructure, cost and technical requirements.

India's energy history is therefore not a story of one fuel simply replacing another. Coal became important as railways and industry expanded, then assumed a central role in electricity generation as the national power system developed. Other energy sources grew alongside it, each contributing in different ways to the country's changing energy requirements.

Today, coal remains a major part of India's energy system, especially in electricity generation and certain industrial processes. At the same time, India's energy mix includes hydropower, nuclear energy, natural gas, solar, wind, petroleum and bioenergy. Their respective contributions vary according to whether the measure is installed capacity, electricity generation or total energy consumption.

Looking at this history helps explain India's present energy system without assuming that any single fuel must disappear. Coal's role reflects more than two centuries of mining, industrial development, infrastructure investment and energy demand. Its place in the future will continue to be shaped by economic requirements, policy decisions, technology and the development of other energy sources.
