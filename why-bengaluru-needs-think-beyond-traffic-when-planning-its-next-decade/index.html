Why Bengaluru Needs to Think Beyond Traffic When Planning Its Next Decade

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Sun, 2 Aug 2026 - 18:08
Opinion

Bengaluru's traffic problem is often reduced to congestion, road capacity and signal management, but the city's mobility challenge is much larger. The question for the next decade should not simply be how to move more vehicles, but how to help people reach work, education, homes and public services efficiently without depending on private vehicles for every journey.

Recent research on Bengaluru's public transport use reinforces this point. A 2026 study published in Frontiers in Sustainable Cities, based on 646 survey responses from Bengaluru, found that travel time, walking distance to transit and the availability of parking strongly influence whether people choose public transport. The study also found that long journeys and inconvenient connections between different modes can significantly discourage public-transport use.

That suggests that simply building more roads may not solve the underlying problem. If a commuter has to walk a long distance to reach a bus or Metro station, wait for an unreliable connection and then spend a long time travelling, owning a car or motorcycle can appear to be the easier option, even when public transport is available.

This is where walkability becomes a transport issue, not merely a pedestrian issue. Bengaluru's Namma Raste programme has explicitly focused on safe, inclusive and pedestrian-friendly streets, while the city's recent Safe Footpath campaign has attempted to reclaim pedestrian space and improve walking conditions.

Public transport also needs to be planned as an interconnected network rather than as separate systems. Bengaluru already has BMTC buses, Namma Metro and suburban-rail-related infrastructure developing around the city, but the journey between these systems matters just as much as the systems themselves.

A commuter should ideally be able to walk safely from home to a bus stop, transfer conveniently to Metro or another service, and complete the final part of the journey without needing a private vehicle. Bengaluru's own climate-action planning has identified measures such as bus-priority lanes, better bus stops, passenger information systems, common ticketing and integrated public-transport information as part of improving public transport.

There is another issue that deserves much more attention: where jobs are located. Bengaluru's technology and employment centres have historically created enormous demand for travel towards particular corridors. Planning jobs, housing and transport independently can force thousands of people into the same corridors every morning and evening.

This is why the idea of bringing jobs closer to mass transit deserves serious consideration. Bengaluru's Namma Raste programme itself included a discussion specifically titled “Bringing Jobs Closer to Mass Transit”, while transit-oriented development proposals have focused on creating denser, mixed-use and more walkable neighbourhoods around mass-transit corridors.

The debate over Bengaluru's proposed double-decker Metro corridors also illustrates the larger question. An IISc technical assessment reported in June 2026 suggested that combining elevated road infrastructure with Metro could potentially increase private-vehicle use and reduce Metro ridership under the scenario studied. That raises an important planning question: should every solution to congestion involve creating additional space for cars?

Of course, Bengaluru still needs good roads. Road maintenance, junction improvements, drainage, traffic enforcement and intelligent signal management remain essential. The argument is not that roads are unimportant; it is that road expansion alone cannot be the city's long-term mobility strategy.

The bigger opportunity is to design Bengaluru around accessibility rather than vehicle movement. A neighbourhood where residents can walk to shops, schools and transit, where employment centres are connected to mass transport, and where buses and Metro work together can reduce the number of journeys that require private vehicles in the first place.

Bengaluru's next decade should therefore be about more than asking “How do we reduce traffic?” The more useful question may be: “Why does so much of Bengaluru's daily life require people to travel so far in the first place?”

In my view, that is where Bengaluru's long-term planning needs to change. A successful technology city should not measure mobility only by how quickly cars move through it, but by how easily people can move without needing a car.

If Bengaluru can combine better public transport, safer footpaths, transit-oriented development, sensible job distribution and well-maintained roads, it can address the causes of congestion rather than repeatedly treating its symptoms.

The city's traffic problem will not disappear with one flyover, one Metro line or one technology platform. The real solution is a city designed so that fewer people are forced into the same congested roads every day. That should be one of the most important planning priorities as Bengaluru looks towards its next decade.
