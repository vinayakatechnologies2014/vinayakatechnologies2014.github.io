Reliance, Venezuelan Oil and India’s Energy Security: Who Benefits When Global Oil Supplies Shift?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Mon, 26 Oct 2026 - 10:11
Verified

India’s energy-security debate has entered a new phase. As international oil prices rise and the rupee faces pressure, the United States is looking towards India’s refining capacity to help expand the market for Venezuelan crude. At the centre of this development is Reliance Industries, led by Mukesh Ambani, and its massive refining complex in Jamnagar, Gujarat.

The development follows the issue examined in Buzz’s earlier report, US Pressures India Over Russian Oil: How New Delhi Is Responding. That story looked at Washington’s pressure on India over Russian oil purchases. The latest developments raise a related question: as the US seeks new destinations for Venezuelan oil, where do India’s energy-security interests fit into the equation?

Why is Reliance central to the discussion?

During his October 2026 visit to India, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio xvisited Reliance’s Jamnagar refining complex and discussed opportunities involving Venezuelan crude. He said Reliance could process more Venezuelan oil and that potential opportunities for the company to participate in oil production in Venezuela could also be explored.

Reliance operates two refineries at Jamnagar with a combined crude-processing capacity of approximately 1.4 million barrels per day. Their sophisticated processing capabilities allow them to handle heavy, high-sulphur crude grades, including Venezuela’s Merey crude.

This gives Reliance a significant commercial advantage when particular grades of oil are available at attractive prices. But processing more Venezuelan oil and investing in oil production in Venezuela are two very different propositions.

Buying crude allows a refinery to evaluate the economics of each shipment. Investing in production would expose a company to additional risks, including political uncertainty, the durability of sanctions arrangements, contractual protections, infrastructure requirements and the reliability of future exports.

The discussions establish that opportunities are being explored; they do not establish that Reliance has committed to a new Venezuelan oil-production investment.

Why does the United States want more Venezuelan oil to reach India?

The US interest is not limited to helping an Indian company find another source of crude. Washington is also trying to reshape energy-trade relationships and expand markets for oil produced in the Western Hemisphere.

Venezuela has enormous oil resources, but increasing production sustainably requires investment, functioning infrastructure, stable commercial arrangements and dependable routes to market. Expanding exports to capable refineries could support that effort.

For India, Venezuelan crude offers another option at a time when the country is managing its purchases from several major suppliers, including Russia, Iraq, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates.

However, this is not simply a matter of replacing one country with another. Oil grades differ in quality, shipping distances affect costs, and not every refinery can process every grade efficiently. The commercial attractiveness of a shipment depends on its delivered price and the value of the products that can be made from it.

That is why Reliance’s refining capability matters. It can process certain heavy grades that many other refineries cannot handle as easily.

Can Venezuelan oil protect India from high crude prices?

This is the central question for India's energy security.

India imports the large majority of the crude oil it consumes. The Petroleum Planning & Analysis Cell has reported oil-import dependency above 87% in recent years. That dependence leaves the country exposed to international price movements, shipping disruptions and geopolitical tensions.

Adding Venezuelan crude to the supply mix could improve diversification. If it is available at a competitive delivered price, it could also help refiners manage their costs.

But a new supplier does not automatically mean cheaper oil for India.

Venezuelan crude must be evaluated against alternatives after accounting for its quality, shipping costs, insurance, refinery-processing requirements and the price of competing grades. A discount at the point of sale may not translate into an equivalent saving once all costs are included.

There is another distinction that matters. India can have a large refining industry and still remain heavily dependent on imported crude. Refining turns crude into usable fuels and other products; it does not mean the crude itself was produced domestically.

India's longer-term energy security therefore depends on several things working together: diversified imports, reliable shipping routes, commercially viable domestic production, strategic reserves, improved energy efficiency and the development of alternative energy sources.

What does this mean for the rupee and bond markets?

The connection is indirect, but important.

When international crude prices rise, India generally needs to spend more on its oil imports unless lower volumes, discounts or other adjustments offset the increase. That can worsen the country's external trade position and increase demand for foreign currency.

On October 23, the rupee closed at ₹94.94 against the US dollar. Government bond yields had also risen, with the benchmark 10-year yield reaching 7.474%. Elevated oil prices were among the pressures markets were watching as the new week began.

These movements cannot be attributed to Reliance's Venezuelan oil plans. The rupee and bond markets are affected by several forces, including global oil prices, US interest-rate expectations, foreign investment flows, inflation expectations and domestic liquidity conditions.

Nevertheless, the wider energy question matters. If India can obtain reliable supplies at competitive prices, it may reduce some of the risks associated with supply disruptions and expensive imports. If global prices remain high regardless of where the oil comes from, changing suppliers alone will not remove the pressure.

Is this a win for Reliance, India or the United States?

The answer depends on the terms of the eventual arrangements and the outcomes they deliver.

Reliance could benefit commercially if it secures competitively priced crude that suits its refineries, improves its product yields or opens profitable investment opportunities. The United States could benefit if more Venezuelan oil reaches international buyers and energy cooperation strengthens its commercial relationship with India.

India could benefit if diversification makes supplies more reliable, lowers the effective cost of imports or reduces exposure to disruptions in a particular region.

But those benefits are not guaranteed to be identical. A deal that is commercially attractive for a refinery does not automatically reduce the price paid by Indian consumers. Nor does a larger refining capacity eliminate the country's vulnerability to international crude prices.

The public-interest test is therefore straightforward: does the arrangement improve India's access to reliable and affordable energy, or does it mainly redirect commercial opportunities between suppliers, refiners and investors?

Answering that question requires examining actual import volumes, prices, contracts and the effects on India's overall energy bill—not merely the announcements made during diplomatic visits.

The questions India should ask next

The coming months should reveal whether the discussions lead to larger Venezuelan crude purchases, concrete investment proposals or simply continued exploration of commercial opportunities.

The important questions are whether India gains more reliable supplies at competitive prices; whether any proposed investment in Venezuelan production has clear financial and legal protections; and whether the government is assessing energy security across the whole economy rather than through the interests of any single company.

India needs private investment and sophisticated refining capacity. It also needs an energy strategy that protects consumers and reduces vulnerability to external shocks.

Reliance’s role in this emerging relationship is worth watching, but the real measure of success will not be the scale of a refinery or the prominence of a diplomatic visit. It will be whether India becomes less vulnerable to oil-price shocks and secures dependable energy at a sustainable cost.
