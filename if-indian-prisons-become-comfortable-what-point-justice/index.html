If Indian Prisons Become Comfortable, What Is the Point of Justice?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Sat, 29 Aug 2026 - 13:07
Opinion

When someone commits a serious crime, the case goes to court. If the person is found guilty, the court can send them to prison.

We understand prison as a punishment. The person loses their freedom and cannot live their normal life.

But what happens after the prison gates close?

If a prisoner gets special comforts, a mobile phone or other facilities that are not allowed, especially through money or influence, then a simple question comes up:

What is the point of the punishment?

What Is Prison Supposed to Do?

Prison is not supposed to be a place where people are treated badly. Prisoners still need food, healthcare, clean surroundings, legal help and basic human dignity. They can also receive education, training and rehabilitation.

But prison is also a place where a person's freedom has been taken away as punishment for a crime.

So there are two things to balance:

  1. Treat prisoners humanely
  2. Make sure the punishment ordered by the court is actually carried out.

One does not cancel the other.

Rights Are Not the Same as Privileges

A prisoner getting medical treatment is a right. Getting food is a basic need. Meeting a lawyer is part of the legal process. Learning a skill or getting counselling can help a prisoner change their life.

But having a secret mobile phone is different. Getting prohibited items is different. Paying someone to break prison rules is different.

A prisoner has rights, but that does not mean every facility outside prison automatically becomes available inside prison.

This difference is important.

What Has Been Happening in Bengaluru?

Recent incidents at Bengaluru's Parappana Agrahara Central Prison have raised questions about how well these rules are being followed.

In October 2025, a prison warden was caught allegedly trying to take a smartphone and two earphones into the prison. Police said he had allegedly been offered ₹10,000 to take the items inside. The investigation was meant to identify the intended recipient and others involved.

In November 2025, videos triggered a controversy over alleged VIP treatment inside the prison. Police later registered an FIR over the allegations.

In March 2026, another incident involving prisoners using mobile phones and other facilities led to action against prison staff. Reports said phones had been hidden near an underground pipeline area.

Then, in August 2026, a CCB raid at the prison reportedly found a 5G smartphone, charger and pen drive in a high-security cell occupied by convicted former MP Prajwal Revanna and another inmate. A prison official was suspended following the discovery.

One incident could be a mistake.

When similar incidents keep happening, people naturally start asking whether there is a bigger problem.

Who Is Responsible?

A prisoner who knowingly breaks prison rules must answer for it.

But prison authorities also have a responsibility. They control security checks, searches, staff movement and access to the prison.

So when a prohibited phone reaches a high-security area, authorities need to find out:

  • Who brought it?
  • How did it enter?
  • Who failed to stop it?
  • Did anyone help?
  • Was money involved?

These questions should be answered through proper investigation, not rumours.

If a prison employee knowingly helps someone break the rules, it becomes a serious problem for the whole system.

What About the Victim?

There is another side that is often forgotten.

Imagine a victim waiting for years for justice. The court finally finds the person guilty and sends them to prison. The victim is told that the person has been punished.

Then the victim sees reports of that person using a secret phone or receiving treatment that appears to go beyond the prison rules.

It is natural to ask:

“Is this really the punishment?”

That does not mean prisoners should be treated cruelly. A victim does not need to see a prisoner suffer unnecessarily.

But a victim does have a right to expect that the punishment ordered by the court is being properly carried out.

A Prison Can Be Humane Without Becoming Permissive

Prisoners should have food. They should receive healthcare. They should have clean living conditions. They should be able to speak to their lawyers. They should have opportunities to study, learn skills and change their lives.

These things do not make justice weaker.

But a secret phone, prohibited items or special treatment obtained through money or influence is different.

Human rights should protect prisoners from cruelty. But they should not become a reason to ignore prison rules.

Opinion: Justice Should Not Have a Price

People in prison have basic rights, and they should be treated fairly. But prison is also a punishment for breaking the law.

We should not want prisons to become places of cruelty. We should want prisoners to receive healthcare, education, rehabilitation and basic dignity.

At the same time, prison authorities must enforce the rules.

  • A mobile phone secretly reaching a high-security cell is not the same as receiving medical treatment.
  • A prison official allegedly taking money to bring prohibited items inside is not the same as rehabilitation.
  • Special treatment because someone is powerful is not the same as respecting human rights.

These things should not be confused.

A court decides a person's punishment. The prison system is responsible for carrying it out.

If money or influence can buy a softer version of that punishment, then we have to ask a very simple question:

Is that really justice?
