Bengaluru’s "One Ward, One Drive": Why Does Routine Civic Work Need a Special Campaign?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Thu, 29 Oct 2026 - 08:58
Public Buzz

Bengaluru’s civic authorities are preparing to launch a month-long “One Ward, One Drive” campaign from November 1 to address potholes, damaged footpaths, drainage problems, garbage accumulation, streetlights and other neighbourhood issues.

The stated objective is straightforward: identify civic problems, coordinate the departments responsible and resolve as many issues as possible by the end of November.

But the announcement raises a more fundamental question: why does Bengaluru need a special campaign to get routine civic work done?

Maintaining roads, clearing drains, collecting waste and ensuring that streetlights function are not additional responsibilities that emerge once a campaign is announced. They are part of the everyday work expected from civic authorities.

Residents do not experience potholes only during a special drive. Garbage does not wait for a government campaign to accumulate. Blocked drains, broken footpaths and faulty streetlights can affect public safety throughout the year.

If these problems require a dedicated month-long campaign to receive attention, the government should explain what has prevented the existing system from addressing them consistently.

The “One Ward, One Drive” initiative could help officials coordinate work, identify neglected locations and clear a backlog of complaints. Bringing different departments together may also help resolve problems that cannot be fixed by one agency acting alone.

But better coordination should be part of normal administration, not something that happens only when a special campaign is announced.

A government that responds to problems only after launching a drive is reacting to its responsibilities rather than fulfilling them consistently.

The real test, therefore, is not how many roads are repaired or how many garbage points are cleared during November. It is whether the campaign identifies why these problems arose, who was responsible for addressing them and what will prevent them from returning.

The Greater Bengaluru Authority and the five city corporations should publish the wards selected for the campaign, the problems identified in each ward, the officers responsible, the work completed and the complaints that remain unresolved.

They should also explain whether the problems were already recorded in existing complaint systems, how long they had remained pending and why they were not resolved earlier.

If a pothole has been reported repeatedly, residents deserve to know why it remained unrepaired. If a drain is cleared only to become blocked again, the administration should examine the underlying cause instead of counting the cleaning exercise as a permanent solution.

The public should not have to wait for a special campaign to receive basic civic services.

There is also a question of what happens after November. If roads, drains and public spaces improve during the drive but deteriorate again a few weeks later, the campaign will have offered temporary relief without fixing the administrative problem.

The government should therefore establish continuing ward-level monitoring, transparent complaint tracking and clear responsibility for recurring problems. Civic performance must be measured throughout the year, not just during a campaign.

Bengaluru needs proactive civic administration. But being proactive does not mean announcing more drives. It means identifying problems early, responding to complaints promptly, maintaining public infrastructure regularly and holding responsible officials accountable when work is neglected.

If the system is functioning as it should, routine civic duties should not require a special campaign. If it is not functioning properly, the government should explain why—and fix the administrative system, not just the visible problems.
