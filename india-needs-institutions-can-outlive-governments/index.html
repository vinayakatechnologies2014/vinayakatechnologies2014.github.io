India Needs Institutions That Can Outlive Governments

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Sun, 20 Sep 2026 - 10:55
Opinion

Human history repeatedly shows that political power is temporary, while institutions can compound influence across generations.

India's own history makes that point better than almost any foreign example.

Think of Nalanda. For centuries, it was not merely a place where people studied. It became a sustained ecosystem of learning, scholarship and intellectual exchange. The Bihar government's historical account places Nalanda's period as a major centre of learning from the fifth century CE to around 1200 CE, flourishing under different periods of political patronage.

It is that the institution became bigger than any individual ruler.

Look at the Asiatic Society in Kolkata, founded in 1784. Over more than two centuries, it built collections, published research, organised lectures and supported scholarship in history, languages, archaeology, humanities and sciences. Its own institutional history says it contributed to the development of organisations including the Archaeological Survey of India, Geological Survey of India, Zoological Survey of India, Botanical Survey of India, Indian Museum and University of Calcutta.

That is what institutional compounding looks like.

One institution creates knowledge.
That knowledge produces scholars.
Scholars create new institutions.
Those institutions train another generation.

The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune offers another example. Founded in 1917, it grew around the study of India's intellectual and textual heritage. Its collections now include more than 28,000 manuscripts and over 1.25 lakh books. It also undertook the monumental Critical Edition of the Mahabharata and developed its own research and publishing activities.

Again, the lesson is bigger than the organisation itself.

Knowledge becomes powerful when it is institutionalised.

The same principle applies to modern science.

C. V. Raman founded the Indian Academy of Sciences in 1934. The Academy was designed not simply to honour scientists but to promote scientific progress, publish research and identify and nurture scientific talent. Decades later, it continues to run programmes connecting students and teachers with scientists and its fellowship.

This is the difference between celebrating a great individual and building an institution that can produce the next generation of great individuals.

India needs much more of this thinking.

We need independent and high-quality publishing houses, research centres, museums, archives, universities, literary journals, film institutions, cultural foundations, scholarship programmes, scientific academies and intellectual forums.

And they need to be strong enough to survive changes in government.

Because governments change.
Political priorities change.
Ministers change.
Majorities change.

But an institution that has a strong reputation, its own intellectual community, serious standards, publications, archives, donors, members and audiences can continue building influence long after the people who founded it are gone.

This is why controlling an existing institution is not the same thing as building one.

India should therefore think beyond the next government and beyond the next election.

And there is another important distinction.

Institution-building should not mean building propaganda machines.

A serious cultural or intellectual institution must be able to produce work that people value without being instructed to value it.

A great publishing house should publish books people actually want to read.
A great university should attract students because of the quality of its scholarship.
A great museum should make people curious.
A great research institution should produce knowledge that survives scrutiny.
A great film institution should produce filmmakers whose work travels beyond political boundaries.

That is how cultural influence becomes durable.

India has already demonstrated that it can build institutions that preserve knowledge for centuries. The question is whether modern India can create the next generation of institutions capable of doing the same for science, literature, cinema, technology, history, philosophy, design and the arts.

The objective should not be to make one ideology permanently powerful.
The objective should be to make Indian intellectual and cultural life institutionally powerful.

Because when political power changes, institutions remain.

And when institutions remain, ideas can continue to evolve, compete, improve and influence generations.

The biggest legacy of a government may therefore not be the policy it announces, but the institutions it leaves behind that no future government needs to rebuild from scratch.

India does not need only governments that can govern for five years. It needs institutions that can build for fifty.
