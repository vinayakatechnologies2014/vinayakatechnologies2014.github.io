In Bengaluru, A Pothole in Our Street: From BBMP to GBA — Who Paid, Who Approved and Who Is Responsible?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Thu, 20 Aug 2026 - 08:01
Public Buzz

You see a pothole in your street. You report it. Eventually, somebody repairs it.

Then, a few months later, the road is dug up again for a water line, electricity work or some other utility. The road is patched once more. And eventually, another pothole appears.

For most of us, the story ends there with:

"Why can't they fix one road properly?"

But who is "they"?

  • Who approved the repair?
  • Who paid for it?
  • Who selected the contractor?
  • Who checked whether the work was done properly?
  • And if the pothole comes back, who is responsible?

To understand that, we need to look at how Bengaluru's system has changed over the past decade.

Before GBA, BBMP was the main municipal government

For many years, BBMP was the main municipal authority responsible for Bengaluru's civic administration.

There was an elected local government.

A simplified picture looked like this:

Citizen → Ward Councillor → BBMP Council → Municipal Administration → Engineer → Contractor

The Commissioner and officials handled the administration and implementation.

The elected councillors and BBMP Council provided the political and democratic side of local government.

But BBMP was never responsible for everything in Bengaluru.

Other organisations such as BDA, BWSSB, BESCOM, BMTC, BMRCL, PWD and Bengaluru Traffic Police had their own responsibilities.

That is why even before GBA, the answer to:

"Who is responsible for this road?"

was sometimes not as simple as saying "BBMP."

But the BBMP breakup idea started much earlier than 2020

This is an important part of the story.

The idea of changing Bengaluru's single large BBMP structure did not suddenly appear in 2020 or 2025.

Around 2015, the Karnataka government was already considering splitting BBMP into smaller municipal corporations.

The basic argument was that Bengaluru had become too large for one corporation to manage effectively.

The thinking was:

Smaller corporations → closer administration → clearer local responsibility → potentially faster decisions.

But there was an obvious problem.

Bengaluru functions as one city.

A road may belong to one local authority, but water, electricity, public transport, drainage and traffic often involve completely different agencies.

So another question immediately appeared:

If Bengaluru is divided into several corporations, who will coordinate the entire city?

That question eventually became central to the GBA model.

Then came 2020 — and something different happened

The BBMP restructuring discussion had already been going on for years.

But in 2020, Bengaluru experienced another major change.

The elected BBMP Council's term ended.

After that, Bengaluru continued to have an administration, engineers, budgets, contractors and civic works.

But there was no elected BBMP Council for several years.

That meant the democratic chain changed.

Before 2020: Citizen → elected councillor → BBMP Council → administration

2020–2025: Citizen → BBMP administration → officials → contractors

There was still government. There were still decisions. There was still public spending.

But there was no elected municipal council.

That distinction is important.

So who approved road repairs during 2020–2025 in Bengaluru?

It would be misleading to say:

"The Commissioner of Bengaluru Approved Everything."

Government spending and contracts operate under laws, financial rules, delegated powers and different levels of approval.

Depending on the work, its value and the applicable rules, different officials or authorities could be involved.

But one thing was clearly different:

There was no elected BBMP Council providing the municipal political layer during this period.

So if a citizen was unhappy with civic administration, they could complain or use administrative, legal or other accountability mechanisms — but there was no elected BBMP councillor or council to hold responsible through a municipal election.

Then came GBA in 2025

After years of discussion about how Bengaluru should be governed, Karnataka enacted the Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2025.

The new system created the Greater Bengaluru Authority (GBA) and a structure involving five City Corporations.

The idea is broadly:

Local matters → City Corporations

Bengaluru-wide coordination → GBA

This is an important change from the old BBMP structure.

But it doesn't mean GBA will personally repair every road.

If the pothole is on a road under a particular City Corporation, that corporation and its administration would generally be the starting point for responsibility.

GBA's larger role is to coordinate the corporations and other agencies on matters affecting Greater Bengaluru as a whole.

Who is at the top of GBA?

This is something many Bengaluru residents may not realise.

The GBA is not headed by a Mayor.

Under the GBA Act:

The Chief Minister of Karnataka is the ex-officio Chairperson of GBA.

The Minister in charge of Bengaluru Development is the ex-officio Vice-Chairperson.

So the structure has a direct connection to the Karnataka state government.

A simplified picture is:

Karnataka Government

↓

Chief Minister — Chairperson, GBA

↓

Greater Bengaluru Authority

↓

Five City Corporations

↓

Local administration

↓

Engineers / contractors

But this doesn't mean the Chief Minister approves every pothole repair.

The point is that GBA itself has state-level political leadership, while local civic administration is intended to operate through the corporations.

What about the Mayors and Councillors of Bengaluru?

This is where the story is still unfinished for Bengaluru.

The new City Corporations are intended to have elected councils and Mayors.

But those elections have not yet taken place in Bengaluru.

The Greater Bengaluru Authority (GBA), along with the Karnataka Government and the Karnataka State Election Commission, asked the Supreme Court for more time to conduct elections for Bengaluru's new city corporations.

The government said it needed more time because of logistical difficulties and the ongoing revision of voter lists. The Supreme Court accepted the request and extended the deadline, directing that the elections be completed by December 31, 2026.

So Bengaluru is currently in an unusual situation:

The new corporation structure exists, but the elected corporation councils are still pending.

Until those elections happen, the normal democratic chain at the new corporation level isn't fully operational.

That means the question:

"Who can I vote out if my corporation performs badly?"

still doesn't have its final answer.

Now let's return to our pothole in Bengaluru

Imagine the pothole is repaired today.

There are several things a citizen should be able to find out.

1. Who is responsible for the road?

Under the old system, it could have been BBMP or another agency.

Today, it could be one of the new City Corporations or another agency depending on the road.

The first question is: Who actually owns or maintains this road?

2. Who approved the money?

Under BBMP, the answer depended on the applicable financial rules, the type and value of the work and delegated powers.

During 2020–2025, the administrative system continued to approve spending even though there was no elected BBMP Council.

Under GBA, financial responsibilities are distributed between the City Corporations, GBA and other government authorities according to the new law, procurement rules and delegated powers.

So there isn't necessarily one person who approves every road repair.

3. Which budget paid for it?

This is where things become interesting.

Was it:

  • BBMP's road budget?
  • A City Corporation budget?
  • A state government allocation?
  • Another agency's budget?

If public money was spent, an ordinary citizen should ideally be able to trace where it came from.

4. Who approved the work?

There can be several stages:

Need identified → Estimate prepared → Technical approval → Financial/administrative approval → Tender or procurement → Work order

The person involved can therefore differ depending on the work and its value.

5. Who selected the contractor?

This should not simply be:

"Someone selected a contractor."

There should be a procurement process governed by applicable rules.

A citizen should ideally be able to find: Tender

  • Who are Bidders?
  • Who won the contract?
  • What is the contract amount?
  • Work order

6. How much was actually paid?

The contract value and the amount eventually paid may not always be exactly the same.

So the useful question isn't just:

"How much was the contract?"

It is:

"How much public money was ultimately paid for the work?"

7. Who checked whether the work was done properly?

Someone needs to verify the work.

  • Was the road repaired according to specifications?
  • Was the work completed?
  • Was the material appropriate?
  • Was the contractor paid after inspection?

These are important questions because a road being patched is not necessarily the same thing as a road being properly repaired.

8. What happens if the pothole comes back?

This is perhaps the most important accountability question.

If the repair fails after a short period:

  • Who complains to the contractor?
  • Can the contractor be required to repair it again?
  • Is there a defect-liability period?
  • Who inspected the original work?
  • Can responsibility be fixed?

This is where the difference between doing a job and being accountable for the job becomes visible.

And then there is the coordination problem in Bengaluru

Suppose the corporation repairs your road properly.

Six months later, another agency needs to dig it up.

For example:

Corporation repairs road

↓

BWSSB carries out water/sewer work

↓

Road is dug up

↓

BESCOM carries out work

↓

Road is dug up again

↓

Another agency works

↓

Road is repaired again

From the citizen's point of view, there is only one result:

The road keeps getting destroyed.

This is one of the reasons GBA matters.

If GBA can coordinate the corporations and other agencies so that road works are planned together, that could be a genuine improvement.

But that has to be demonstrated in practice.

So what actually changed in Bengaluru?

The simplest way to look at Bengaluru's recent history is:

2015 onwards - Debate about restructuring BBMP

  • The idea of splitting Bengaluru's municipal administration was already being discussed.

2020–2025 - BBMP without an elected council

  • The administration continued, but the elected municipal political layer was absent.

2025 onwards - GBA + five City Corporations

  • A new governance structure was created.

2026 - Still waiting for elected corporation councils

  • The Supreme Court has extended the election deadline to December 31, 2026.

So, is GBA actually making Bengaluru more accountable?

That is the question we cannot answer simply by looking at the new organisational chart.

The real test is whether a citizen can now follow one ordinary civic problem from beginning to end.

For our pothole:

Who is responsible?

Who approved it?

Where did the money come from?

Who got the contract?

How much was paid?

Who inspected it?

Was the work actually completed properly?

What happens when it fails?

And finally:

Who can the citizen hold responsible — administratively and politically?

If those answers become easier to find, the new system may be improving accountability.

If the citizen still has to move between BBMP/GBA, corporations, BDA, BWSSB, BESCOM, PWD and other agencies just to find out who is responsible, then the fundamental problem of fragmented responsibility may still remain.

The Public Buzz question

We don't need to begin by saying GBA is good. We don't need to say GBA is bad. And we don't need to allege corruption.

We can start with something much simpler:

There is a pothole in our street.

Then follow the documents.

  • Who owns the road?
  • Who approved the repair?
  • Where did the money come from?
  • Who got the contract?
  • Who inspected it?
  • Who paid?
  • And if the pothole comes back — who is responsible?

That one pothole could tell us a lot about how Bengaluru has been governed, how it is governed today, and whether GBA actually makes government more accountable to the people who live here.
