If Software Is Government Infrastructure, Why Is Bengaluru Charging Citizens a Separate ₹200 Software Charge?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Sat, 19 Sep 2026 - 12:39
Opinion

Bengaluru property owners are reportedly being asked to pay a separate ₹200 “software charge” for residential properties and ₹1,000 for commercial properties along with property tax.

The charge raises a bigger question:

If software is already part of government infrastructure, why are citizens being charged separately for it?

Bengaluru's property-tax system is not a new private platform. BBMP's official IT records show that its property-tax ecosystem, including GEPTIS and the online property-tax system, has been part of the civic administration for years. The IT department is also responsible for software, servers, data-centre maintenance and related IT infrastructure.

Karnataka already runs a large network of government digital systems, including e-Swathu, e-Aasthi and several departmental applications through its e-governance infrastructure. GBA/BBMP itself operates multiple digital services for property tax, building permissions, trade licences, GIS and other civic functions.

So why does property tax now need a separate software charge?

The bigger issue is the money trail.

If ₹200 is being collected specifically for software, citizens should be able to know:

  • Who authorised the charge?
  • Where is the official order?
  • Which government account or revenue head receives the money?
  • Who currently develops and maintains the property-tax system?
  • Is there a separate software or maintenance contract?
  • How much does the government already spend on the system from the municipal budget?

The official property-tax portal currently lists its circulars and orders, but a specific publicly listed order establishing the ₹200 residential or ₹1,000 commercial software charge has not been found.

That does not by itself prove the charge is unauthorised. But it does leave a basic question unanswered:

What exactly are Bengaluru's property owners being asked to pay for?

And there is a wider question for Karnataka's digital-government model.

If government software is treated as public infrastructure for one service, should citizens have to separately finance it whenever they use another government service?

₹200 may be a small amount for one taxpayer. But across a large property-tax base, even a small charge can become significant.

The issue, therefore, is not simply ₹200.

It is transparency over who collects it, why it is collected, what it funds and where the money goes.
