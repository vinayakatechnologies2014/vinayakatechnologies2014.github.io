Election Commission Defends SIR Before Supreme Court. What Does It Mean for Voters?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Tue, 27 Oct 2026 - 09:39
Developing

The Election Commission says its decisions on voter-list revision were approved by all three commissioners. But questions remain about the process, the documents required from voters and what happens when an eligible voter’s name is missing.

The Election Commission of India (ECI) is facing questions over its Special Intensive Revision (SIR), an exercise to check and update electoral rolls.

The controversy is not just about political parties protesting against SIR. It also concerns how decisions were taken within the Election Commission, how voter-registration requirements were introduced and whether the process could make it difficult for some eligible citizens to register.

Some of these concerns have reached the Supreme Court.

One petition alleged that Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar took decisions in the name of the Election Commission without the approval of the other two Election Commissioners, Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi. It also questioned aspects of the SIR process and the additional declaration linked to Form 6, the application used by people registering as voters for the first time.

The petition sought judicial scrutiny of these decisions and challenged the legality of the process. These are allegations before the Court, not findings that wrongdoing has been established.

On October 26, the Election Commission filed its response before the Supreme Court, rejecting the allegations and defending its decisions.
(Note: The Supreme Court’s bench, led by the Chief Justice of India, is actively hearing this batch of petitions and has yet to issue its final verdict.)

To understand the dispute, here is a simple comparison of what was alleged and how the Commission responded.

Claims raised before the Supreme CourtElection Commission’s defence
1. Decisions allegedly taken without the approval of the other two Election Commissioners. The petition challenged whether decisions were taken in the name of the Commission without proper collective approval.The ECI said all three major SIR orders were approved unanimously. It argued that questions or objections raised during file examination did not mean the final decisions were taken by one commissioner alone.
2. Concerns over the additional declaration linked to Form 6. Critics questioned whether voter-registration requirements had effectively been changed without following the proper procedure.The ECI said Form 6 itself had not been amended and the declaration was a separate document. It acknowledged that the online presentation could cause confusion and said the documents had been displayed separately from October 21.
3. Difficulties in linking applicants to earlier voter lists. Eligible voters whose own or family records were difficult to find could face practical problems.The ECI said Booth Level Officers (BLOs) could collect documents from affected voters at their homes, reducing the need for them to attend hearings in person.
4. Questions about the legality of the decisions and procedures. The petition asked the Court to examine whether the Commission had acted within its legal authority.The ECI defended its decisions as lawful and asked the Court to reject the allegations.

This table summarises the competing positions. It does not mean the allegations have been proved or that the Supreme Court has accepted the ECI’s defence.

One detail in the Commission’s response deserves attention. The ECI maintained that it had not changed Form 6, but acknowledged that the way the additional declaration was displayed online could give people the impression that the form itself had been amended.

That distinction matters. Even when an organisation believes its rules are correct, the way those rules are presented can create confusion. The Commission says it addressed the issue by separating the online application and declaration.

But what does all this mean for an ordinary voter?

Imagine someone who has voted before but discovers that their name is missing from the electoral roll. They need to know why it is missing, which documents are required, where to submit them and whether the problem can be corrected before an election.

A first-time voter may face a different difficulty if the required links to older electoral records cannot easily be established. For both, the most important question is whether the process is clear and whether officials can resolve genuine problems in time.

A voter should not be left struggling to understand a procedure when their right to vote depends on getting it right.

There is another related concern before the Supreme Court. In a separate case concerning Maharashtra, petitioners raised questions about deleted voters being asked to use Form 6 to seek re-entry into the electoral roll. On October 26, the Court assured them that they would not be prosecuted for applying to register again and sought the ECI’s response. The matter was listed for November 5.

This is a separate legal proceeding from the challenge to the Commission’s internal decision-making. However, it raises a practical question for citizens: if an eligible voter’s name is removed by mistake, is there a clear, safe and timely way to restore it?

It is also important to distinguish between raising concerns and proving wrongdoing. A political party or protest group making an allegation does not automatically make it true. At the same time, the Election Commission’s denial does not, by itself, settle every legal or procedural question.

The Supreme Court will have to consider the evidence and arguments before it. The ECI’s affidavit explains the Commission’s position, but it is not a final judicial ruling on the allegations.

For citizens, the real test is straightforward. Are eligible voters being included? Can people understand the requirements? Can mistakes be corrected in time? And are the safeguards working in practice?

The Election Commission has defended its decisions, but the wider dispute remains under examination. The focus should now be on whether the process protects every eligible citizen from being wrongly left off the voter list.
