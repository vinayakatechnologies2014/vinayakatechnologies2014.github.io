Is India’s Growing Dependence on Russian Oil Becoming an Energy Security Risk?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Sun, 23 Aug 2026 - 10:14
Opinion

India's relationship with imported crude oil has changed significantly in recent years. Russia has become an important source of crude for Indian refiners, giving the country access to another major supplier at a time when global oil markets have been heavily influenced by geopolitical tensions.

For India, the attraction is understandable. Competitive crude supplies can help refiners manage costs, support domestic fuel availability and reduce some pressure on the country's import bill.

But there is a bigger question behind the Russian-oil story:

Can relying heavily on one major supplier eventually become an energy-security risk, even when that supplier offers oil at attractive prices?

The answer is not simply yes or no.

Russian oil itself is not necessarily a threat to India's energy security. The real concern is concentration. A strong energy strategy requires India to retain enough alternatives so that a disruption involving any one supplier, shipping route or geopolitical bloc does not threaten the wider economy.

And this is where India's current position is more resilient than the phrase "dependence on Russian oil" might suggest.

India's Oil Problem Is Bigger Than Russia

India is one of the world's largest consumers of crude oil, while domestic production cannot meet national demand.

PPAC's petroleum data shows the scale of India's structural dependence on imported crude. Its March 2025 oil-and-gas snapshot put crude-oil import dependency at around 88% on a petroleum-products-consumption basis.

That number explains why crude procurement is such an important economic and strategic issue.

Oil is not confined to petrol and diesel. Petroleum products support transportation, aviation, agriculture, manufacturing, logistics and numerous industrial activities. A major disruption in crude supplies can therefore move far beyond the energy sector.

For India, the objective cannot simply be to find the cheapest available barrel.

It has to secure - reliable, affordable and diversified supplies.

That is the context in which Russian crude became increasingly important.

Why Russia Became Important

Russia was not always one of India's most important crude suppliers.

The global oil market changed substantially after the Russia-Ukraine conflict. Changes in international trade patterns created opportunities for Indian refiners to purchase Russian crude.

India's approach has been largely driven by its energy requirements and commercial considerations. From an Indian perspective, purchasing crude from a major producing country can make sense when the economics, logistics and refinery requirements work in its favour.

The important point, however, is that buying Russian oil and depending exclusively on Russian oil are two very different things.

India's energy strategy does not have to choose one supplier over another.

It needs to preserve the ability to choose among many suppliers.

India's Biggest Defence: Supplier Diversification

This is where the discussion becomes more nuanced.

India has substantially expanded its crude-supplier base.

The Government of India stated in March 2026 that India was sourcing crude from 40 countries, compared with 27 countries in 2006-07.

That diversification has practical value.

If one producing country experiences a disruption, India can look elsewhere. If one shipping route becomes unavailable, alternative routes can become more important. If a particular grade of crude becomes expensive, refiners can adjust their procurement depending on refinery compatibility and market conditions.

The government had already emphasised diversification before the latest disruptions. In 2021, the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas said Indian oil PSUs had diversified their crude basket across regions including the Middle East, Africa, North America and South America, specifically to reduce the risk of dependence on a single region.

This is an important distinction when discussing Russian oil.

India's energy-security strategy is broader than Russia.

Russia is one supplier within a much larger procurement network.

The Strait of Hormuz Shows Why Diversification Matters

The most useful test of India's energy resilience came during the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz in March 2026.

Before the crisis, approximately 45% of India's crude imports transited the Hormuz route, according to the Petroleum Ministry. During the disruption, India increased procurement through alternative sources, with non-Hormuz sourcing rising to approximately 70% of crude imports.

The government also stated that India was receiving crude from around 40 countries and that the volumes secured through diversified procurement exceeded what would normally have arrived through the disrupted route.

That experience offers an important lesson.

Energy security is not about finding one perfect supplier. It is about having enough alternatives when conditions suddenly change.

And that is precisely why excessive dependence on any individual supplier should be avoided — whether that supplier is Russia, a Gulf producer or another major oil exporter.

The Real Risk Is Concentration

Russian oil can actually contribute to India's energy security when it forms part of a diversified procurement strategy.

The risk begins when the share of any single supplier becomes so large that replacing it becomes difficult or expensive.

Imagine a situation in which geopolitical developments suddenly make Russian crude harder to purchase. India would not necessarily face an immediate physical shortage because it has other suppliers and refining capacity.

But replacing a large volume quickly could come at a higher cost.

Alternative crude can involve different transportation distances, insurance costs, grades and commercial terms. Indian refineries can process a wide variety of crude, but that flexibility does not mean every replacement barrel will cost the same.

So the question is not:

"Can India survive without Russian oil?"

It almost certainly has alternatives.

The more useful question is:

"How much would it cost India to replace Russian oil if it suddenly had to?"

That is where concentration becomes an energy-security issue.

India's Import Dependence Remains the Bigger Vulnerability

There is another issue that can easily get lost in the debate over Russian crude.

Even if India reduced its Russian imports substantially, it would still need to import large quantities of crude.

In other words, replacing Russian oil would not eliminate India's fundamental energy vulnerability.

It would simply change the source of the imported oil.

That is why India's energy strategy has to operate on two levels.

First, diversify crude suppliers and transportation routes.

Second, gradually reduce the country's structural dependence on imported fossil fuels.

The first can happen relatively quickly through procurement decisions.

The second requires years of investment and changes in consumption patterns.

Alternative Energy Can Reduce the Pressure

India cannot eliminate crude oil from its economy overnight.

Millions of vehicles, industries, farms and businesses continue to rely on liquid fuels.

But every reduction in petroleum demand can eventually reduce exposure to international crude markets.

India has been expanding ethanol blending, natural-gas infrastructure, renewable energy and other alternatives as part of its broader energy transition.

Government data shows that ethanol blending has risen dramatically over the past decade. Average blending under the Ethanol Blended Petrol programme reached about 19.2% in ESY 2024-25, with the government subsequently reporting 20% for ESY 2025-26 through June 2026.

The Government of India's January 2026 energy-transition backgrounder also highlighted the expansion of India's natural-gas pipeline network and the continuing importance of energy diversification.

These measures will not make crude oil irrelevant.

But they can reduce the amount of imported petroleum India needs over time.

Strategic Reserves Add Another Layer of Protection

India's energy resilience is also supported by strategic petroleum reserves.

Phase I of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve programme established storage facilities at Visakhapatnam, Mangaluru and Padur, with a combined capacity of about 5.33 million tonnes.

The purpose of these reserves is not to permanently replace imports.

They are a buffer.

If international supplies are disrupted, reserves can provide additional time for the government and oil companies to secure alternative supplies and manage the disruption.

This becomes particularly valuable for a country with such a high level of crude import dependence.

India's Refining Capacity Is Another Advantage

India's position is also strengthened by its substantial refining capacity.

The country does not simply import crude and consume it domestically. Its refineries process crude into petroleum products, and India is also a major exporter of refined petroleum products.

During the March 2026 disruption, the government highlighted the high utilisation of Indian refineries and their role in maintaining petroleum-product availability.

That refining capability gives India an important layer of flexibility.

It means the country's energy-security equation is not determined solely by where each barrel of crude originates.

It also depends on how efficiently India can convert available crude into the fuels its economy needs.

What If Russian Oil Becomes More Difficult to Buy?

This is the scenario worth watching.

If geopolitical developments, sanctions, shipping restrictions or commercial conditions make Russian crude significantly harder to purchase, India could seek replacement supplies from other producing regions.

The country has already demonstrated that it can broaden procurement.

But replacement is not necessarily cost-neutral.

If global crude prices are simultaneously rising, replacing Russian volumes could increase India's import bill. Higher crude prices can eventually affect transportation costs, inflation and the wider economy.

This is why maintaining multiple supply relationships is so important.

Diversification gives India options.

Options have economic value even when they are not immediately used.

It Is Not a Choice Between Russia and the West

The Russian-oil debate is sometimes presented as though India has to choose between Russian supplies and Western suppliers.

That is too narrow a way to look at energy security.

India's objective should be to maintain relationships with as many commercially and strategically viable sources as possible.

Russia can be one.

The Gulf can be another.

Suppliers in the Americas, Africa and other regions can provide additional alternatives.

The Government of India's own procurement strategy demonstrates this approach: India's crude-supply network has expanded from 27 countries to around 40.

For India, strategic autonomy in energy means having choices.

India Should Think Beyond the Next Barrel

The most important energy-security question is not where India's next shipment of crude will come from.

It is how much crude India will need to import in the future.

Every barrel that India can avoid importing reduces its exposure to international price movements, shipping disruptions and geopolitical uncertainty.

That makes energy efficiency, electrification, alternative fuels, domestic production and renewable energy more than environmental policies.

They are also energy-security policies.

But this transition has to be realistic.

India will continue to need petroleum for many years. The immediate objective should therefore be to make imported oil supplies more resilient while gradually reducing the country's dependence on them.

Verdict: Russian Oil Is an Opportunity, But Concentration Is the Risk

So, is India's growing use of Russian oil becoming an energy-security risk?

Not necessarily.

Russian crude can strengthen India's energy position when it provides a commercially attractive and reliable source within a diversified procurement strategy.

The risk appears when dependence on any one supplier becomes excessive.

India's strongest defence is therefore not choosing Russia over another country.

It is maintaining the ability to choose Russia, Gulf producers, suppliers from the Americas, Africa and other regions according to market conditions and India's national interest.

The country's expansion from 27 crude-sourcing countries to around 40, its ability to increase non-Hormuz sourcing during a major disruption, its refining capacity and its strategic reserves all provide important layers of resilience.

But there is still a deeper vulnerability.

India remains heavily dependent on imported crude.

That means the long-term goal cannot simply be to diversify where the oil comes from.

It must also be to reduce how much imported oil the country needs.

The real question for India's energy future is therefore not:

"Should India buy Russian oil?"

It is:

"Can India build an energy system in which no single country, shipping route or fuel source can seriously threaten the country's economic stability?"

That is the real measure of energy security.

For now, Russian oil is better understood as one component of India's energy strategy — not the strategy itself.

And India's smartest path forward is clear: keep the options open, keep the supply diversified and steadily reduce the dependence that makes every international oil shock matter.
