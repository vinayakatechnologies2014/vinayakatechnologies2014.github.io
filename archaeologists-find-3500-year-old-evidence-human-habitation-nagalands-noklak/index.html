Archaeologists Find 3,500-Year-Old Evidence of Human Habitation in Nagaland's Noklak

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Mon, 24 Aug 2026 - 12:03
Verified

Archaeological investigations in Nagaland's Noklak district have uncovered evidence indicating that people were using a rock shelter in the region around 3,500 years ago, providing new evidence about prehistoric human habitation in Northeast India.

The findings came from archaeological work carried out in the Chiphur area of Noklak district, where researchers explored caves and rock shelters in the Naga Hills Ophiolite Belt. The work was conducted by archaeologists from the Department of History & Archaeology at Nagaland University together with the Department of Art & Culture, Government of Nagaland.

Nagaland University identifies prehistoric archaeology and the study of early Naga ancestral sites as important areas of research for its Department of History & Archaeology. The university also records ongoing archaeological collaboration with the state Department of Art & Culture.

What Was Found in Noklak?

During the archaeological expedition, researchers surveyed rugged limestone terrain in the Chiphur area and identified five caves and four rock shelters.

The investigation produced several forms of archaeological evidence, including:

  • Cord-marked pottery
  • Ground stone tools
  • Hearth remains
  • Carbonised plant remains
  • Faunal remains

Among the sites examined was a rock shelter identified as Leilo Kaa.

Scientific dating of material associated with the site indicates that Leilo Kaa was used approximately 3,500 years ago.

A separate cave site, Kathso Kaa, produced evidence dating to approximately 1,000 years ago.

The results therefore indicate that caves and rock shelters in this part of present-day Nagaland were used by human communities across different periods of the region's past.

How Was the Age Determined?

The age of the archaeological material was not simply estimated from the appearance of the pottery or tools.

Researchers used Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) dating, a scientific method used to establish the age of suitable archaeological material.

The dating work produced two AMS dates for the investigated cave and rock-shelter sites. The results provided chronological evidence for prehistoric occupation and helped establish the approximate age of the sites.

Nagaland University's Department of History & Archaeology lists Beta Analytic among its research collaborators for radiocarbon and AMS dating, alongside institutions involved in archaeological, archaeobotanical and geoarchaeological research.

Why Is 3,500 Years Significant?

The discovery matters because archaeological evidence from Northeast India is still considerably less extensive than evidence available from some other parts of the Indian subcontinent.

Northeast India sits in a geographically important position between South Asia and Southeast Asia. Archaeologists have long considered the region significant for understanding ancient population movement, settlement and the development of early subsistence practices.

The newly investigated sites add more evidence to that archaeological picture.

The findings also provide clues about how prehistoric communities used the landscape. Pottery, stone tools, hearth remains, plant remains and animal remains can collectively help researchers investigate questions about food, technology, settlement patterns and the relationship between people and their environment.

Nagaland University's Department of History & Archaeology says its interdisciplinary research has produced direct archaeological evidence concerning early subsistence strategies and is helping researchers understand the archaeological record of the Mid-Holocene and Late Holocene periods in the Naga Hills and Northeast India.

What Did People Living There Do?

The evidence does not allow researchers to reconstruct every aspect of the lives of the people who used Leilo Kaa.

However, the combination of pottery, stone tools, hearth remains and carbonised plant material provides important clues.

Carbonised cereals identified in the investigation include Setaria italica, commonly known as foxtail millet, and Coix species, commonly associated with Job's tears. Wild faunal remains were also recovered.

Such evidence can help archaeologists investigate the subsistence practices of ancient communities and determine how people used plants, animals and locally available resources.

The pottery and stone tools can also provide information about technological practices and cultural connections.

A Larger Archaeological Picture

The Noklak findings are part of a much broader body of archaeological research being conducted in Nagaland.

Earlier archaeological research in the state's cave and rock-shelter landscapes has already demonstrated that these environments contain evidence from considerably earlier periods.

A government-hosted archaeological research record from the Tribal Research Institute, Nagaland, documents previous cave archaeology research examining prehistoric human migration, occupation and the development of agriculture and settlements in the Naga Hills.

That research also records archaeological evidence including pottery, stone tools, faunal remains and human burials at other cave and rock-shelter sites in Nagaland.

The new Noklak findings therefore do not stand alone. Instead, they add another important set of evidence to a growing archaeological record of prehistoric occupation in the region.

Does This Rewrite Northeast India's History?

It is safer to say that the discovery extends the archaeological evidence for human occupation in this part of Northeast India rather than claiming that it completely rewrites the region's history.

The researchers have identified evidence dating to around 3,500 years ago at Leilo Kaa. Further excavation, dating and analysis will be needed to establish a more complete picture of the people who used the site and their relationship with other prehistoric communities in Northeast India and neighbouring regions.

Importantly, the discovery demonstrates how much remains to be investigated.

Nagaland University's Department of History & Archaeology says its continuing exploration of cave and rock-shelter sites in the Ophiolite region could lead to the identification of additional sites of greater antiquity.

What Happens Next?

Archaeological research in Noklak is expected to continue.

The researchers' findings provide a basis for further investigation of the caves and rock shelters in the area, including additional scientific dating and analysis of artefacts and environmental remains.

The discoveries also highlight the importance of protecting archaeological sites in landscapes that remain relatively unexplored.

For now, the most significant confirmed finding is straightforward:

Archaeological evidence from the Leilo Kaa rock shelter indicates human use of the site approximately 3,500 years ago.

That evidence adds an important new piece to the archaeological record of Nagaland and contributes to the broader effort to understand the prehistoric history of Northeast India.
