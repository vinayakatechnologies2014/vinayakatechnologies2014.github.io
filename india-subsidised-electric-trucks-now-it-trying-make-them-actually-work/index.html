India Subsidised Electric Trucks. Now It Is Trying to Make Them Actually Work

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Tue, 8 Sep 2026 - 09:27
Verified

India has already started subsidising electric trucks. The next problem is much harder: making electric freight commercially viable at scale.

On September 7, NITI Aayog launched the Platform for Aggregating Clean Transport (PACT) and the Zero Emission Truck (ZET) Marketplace at the 5th e-FAST India Summit.

The move is significant because the government is no longer focusing only on helping companies buy electric trucks. It is trying to connect the entire system needed to operate them — freight companies, truck manufacturers, financiers, charging operators and technology providers.

The reason is straightforward. India already has more than 3,000 electric medium- and heavy-duty trucks in operation, and deployments of electric freight vehicles increased more than fourfold, from 201 in FY2025 to 826 in FY2026. But the market is still small compared with India's enormous road-freight sector.

According to NITI Aayog, 70% of India's freight moves by road. Heavy trucks account for only around 3–4% of the country's vehicle fleet but contribute more than one-third of transport-sector carbon emissions.

That explains why the government is concentrating on trucks rather than treating all electric vehicles in the same way.

India already has a demand-side incentive under PM E-DRIVE. The scheme provides an incentive of up to ₹9.6 lakh per electric truck and is designed to support approximately 5,600 e-trucks. The government has also made scrapping an old polluting truck mandatory for receiving the incentive.

But cheaper purchase prices do not solve the whole problem.

A logistics operator also needs affordable financing, reliable charging and enough freight demand to keep the vehicle running. If an electric truck is sitting idle because there is no suitable charging point on its route, the economics quickly become difficult.

This is where PACT is different.

The platform will aggregate freight demand and identify deployment opportunities along specific freight corridors. It is intended to give truck manufacturers and charging companies better visibility about where demand actually exists, while giving fleet operators and financiers more certainty about potential projects.

The ZET Marketplace works alongside it by connecting e-truck manufacturers, logistics companies, charging operators, financiers and technology providers.

There is also an energy-security argument.

The government says freight electrification can help reduce dependence on petroleum while improving energy security. NITI Aayog has previously examined the potential for large-scale zero-emission trucking to reduce India's oil-import requirements over the long term.

But this is not yet a solved problem.

NITI Aayog itself identifies high financing costs, fragmented logistics operations and insufficient coordinated corridor planning as major barriers. The government is now asking the market to help solve those problems through PACT and the ZET Marketplace.

For ordinary people, the outcome matters even if they never drive an electric truck.

Trucks carry food, consumer goods, industrial products and parcels across the country. If electric freight eventually lowers operating and logistics costs, improves air quality and reduces petroleum dependence, the effects can eventually reach consumers.

But that benefit is not immediate. PACT has just been launched.

The real test will be whether India can turn the new platform into actual electric freight corridors, affordable financing, reliable charging and enough truck deployments to make the business case work without depending permanently on subsidies.
