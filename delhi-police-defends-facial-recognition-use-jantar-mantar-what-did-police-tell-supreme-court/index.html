Delhi Police Defends Facial Recognition Use at Jantar Mantar: What Did the Police Tell the Supreme Court?

Submitted by Karthik Kumar D K on Wed, 19 Aug 2026 - 12:04
Developing

Delhi Police has told the Supreme Court that the facial-recognition technology used during the recent student protests at Jantar Mantar was not deployed to conduct indiscriminate surveillance of protesters.

In an affidavit filed before the Supreme Court, the police said its facial-recognition system identified 2,873 people whose faces matched existing police records during the protest period. The police maintained that the system was being used to identify people with serious criminal antecedents and not to create profiles of every person attending the demonstration.

The issue is now before the Supreme Court because a separate petition challenges the use of facial-recognition technology and related biometric-surveillance measures at protest sites.

That means there are two things readers need to keep separate: what Delhi Police says happened, and what the Supreme Court ultimately decides.

What did Delhi Police tell the Supreme Court?

According to its affidavit, Delhi Police rejected allegations that the facial-recognition system was being used for mass surveillance.

The police said the system was used to compare images captured at the protest with existing police databases containing records of people with serious criminal backgrounds.

It also rejected claims that the technology was being used to collect biometric information indiscriminately, obtain people's Aadhaar details or sell their data to private entities.

The police's position is that the technology was a targeted law-enforcement tool rather than a system designed to monitor everyone at the protest.

How many people did the police identify?

Delhi Police told the Supreme Court that 2,873 people were identified through the facial-recognition system between July 20 and July 26.

According to information reported from police records, the figure consisted of:

  • 2,402 people matched through the Crime-Kundli database.
  • 471 people matched through dossier records.

The police also said 92 of the 2,873 people had involvement in more than 10 cases, while 47 were classified as history-sheeters.

These numbers are important, but they need to be understood correctly.

Does 2,873 mean 2,873 protesters committed crimes?

No.

The figure does not establish that 2,873 people committed offences during the Jantar Mantar protest.

It represents people whom Delhi Police says were identified because their faces matched existing police records.

A previous report based on a document tabled in Parliament also noted that the records did not establish that the identified individuals had committed violence during the protest. The document did not specify whether every facial-recognition match resulted in an arrest, preventive action or further investigation.

That distinction is particularly important.

A person having a criminal case or police record is not the same thing as that person being convicted of a crime, and neither does it establish that the person committed an offence at the protest.

What types of criminal records were involved?

The police records reportedly included people associated with cases involving serious offences.

The categories included murder, attempted murder, dacoity and robbery, rape, offences under the POCSO Act, molestation, arms-related offences, snatching, kidnapping and narcotics-related offences.

The largest category reportedly consisted of people booked under various provisions of the Indian Penal Code, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and other special laws.

Again, these are records associated with the individuals identified by the system. They should not be presented as proof that those individuals committed offences during the Jantar Mantar protest.

Was facial recognition the only basis for police action?

Delhi Police says it was not.

The police told the Supreme Court that facial-recognition identification was followed by field verification.

In other words, according to the police's account, a facial match was not supposed to automatically establish that a person was a suspect or automatically trigger police action.

The police also said that its investigation would focus on people among the 2,873 identified individuals who had serious criminal charges or antecedents.

This is the police's stated procedure. Whether the actual use of the system and the safeguards surrounding it meet constitutional and legal requirements is a separate issue now before the Court.

Why is the Supreme Court involved?

A separate Article 32 petition has been filed challenging the deployment of facial-recognition technology and related biometric-surveillance measures by police at protest sites.

The petition was filed by Rajya Sabha MP A.A. Rahim and challenges what it describes as indiscriminate biometric surveillance at peaceful assemblies.

The case is listed as:

A.A. Rahim M.P. v. Union of India
Diary No. 45049/2026

The Supreme Court agreed to hear the petition and tagged it with other matters concerning the recent student protests.

The Supreme Court's involvement is significant because the question is not simply whether facial recognition can help police identify people with criminal records.

The larger question is what legal and constitutional safeguards should apply when such technology is used at a public demonstration.

What is the concern about privacy?

Facial recognition is different from ordinary observation by police officers.

The technology can compare a person's face against a database and potentially link that person to existing records.

When this happens at a large public gathering, questions arise about:

  • Who is being scanned?
  • Which database is being used?
  • What information is retained?
  • How long is it retained?
  • Who can access the information?
  • Can the information be shared with other agencies?
  • What happens when the system produces a wrong match?
  • What safeguards protect people who are peacefully attending a protest?

These are the issues that make the case important beyond Jantar Mantar.

The petition before the Supreme Court specifically challenges the use of facial-recognition technology and allied biometric-surveillance measures at protest sites.

What has the Supreme Court actually ruled?

This is where headlines can become misleading.

The Supreme Court has not, as of August 19, 2026, issued a final ruling declaring Delhi Police's use of facial recognition at Jantar Mantar either legal or illegal.

The Court has agreed to hear the challenge concerning facial recognition and biometric surveillance.

The broader Jantar Mantar proceedings are also continuing.

The principal related matter concerning allegations of police action against protesters is:

Shailendra Mani Tripathi v. Union of India & Ors.
W.P. (Crl.) No. 280/2026
Diary No. 44078/2026

These are separate but connected developments and should not be presented as though the Supreme Court has already delivered a final judgment on facial recognition.

What is confirmed and what is not?

Confirmed

  • Delhi Police has acknowledged in its Supreme Court affidavit that facial-recognition technology was used during the Jantar Mantar student protests.
  • Delhi Police told the Court that the system identified 2,873 people whose faces matched existing police records during July 20–26.
  • The police said 2,402 matches came through the Crime-Kundli database and 471 through dossier records.
  • The police said 92 people identified had involvement in more than 10 cases and 47 were history-sheeters.
  • The Supreme Court is considering a petition challenging the use of facial-recognition and related biometric-surveillance technology at protest sites.

Not established by the Court

  • It has not been established that all 2,873 people committed an offence during the protest.
  • It has not been established that every person identified was arrested because of facial recognition.
  • It has not been established that every facial-recognition match was accurate.
  • It has not been established that Delhi Police's deployment of the technology violated the Constitution.
  • And the Supreme Court has not yet delivered a final ruling settling the legality of facial-recognition surveillance at protests.

Why this story matters

The Jantar Mantar case is becoming an important test of how technology and civil liberties interact.

For the police, facial recognition can be a tool for locating people with serious criminal histories in a large crowd.

For civil-liberty advocates, scanning people at a protest can create a different problem: the possibility that people participating peacefully in a public demonstration could be identified, recorded or linked to government databases without sufficient safeguards.

The Supreme Court will ultimately have to consider where that line should be drawn.

For now, the most accurate description is neither that “Delhi Police caught 2,873 criminals” nor that “the Supreme Court has declared facial recognition illegal.”

The verified facts are narrower:

Delhi Police used facial-recognition technology during the Jantar Mantar protests, says it identified 2,873 people by matching them against existing records, and has defended that use before the Supreme Court. A separate Supreme Court petition challenging facial-recognition and biometric surveillance at protest sites is now being heard. The final legal position has not yet been decided.

Current status

The underlying events and Delhi Police's court filing are established. However, the constitutional and legal questions surrounding the use of facial-recognition technology at protests remain before the Supreme Court.
